Risk ratio (\(RR\))

\(Risk_{tr}\) (to be dead in case of treatment) = \(\dfrac{Dead | Treatment}{Total\ Treatment}\)

risk_tr <- contingency_table_big['Treatment', 'Dead']/ contingency_table_big['Treatment', 'Sum']
risk_tr
[1] 0.4

\(Risk_{pl}\) (to be dead in case of Placebo) = \(\dfrac{Dead | Placebo}{Total\ Placebo}\) =

risk_pl <- contingency_table_big['Placebo', 'Dead']/ contingency_table_big['Placebo', 'Sum']
risk_pl
[1] 0.625

Risk ratio (\(RR\)) = \(\dfrac{Risk_{pl}}{Risk_{tr}} =\)

RR <- risk_pl / risk_tr
RR
[1] 1.5625

There is some association between Treatment and Result - Placebo patients tend to be dead more often.

NB! The group we are comparing with is usually in the denominator!

risk_pl_1

RR

Almost the same \(RR\), despite big number of cured!

What changes when we transpose the table?

The same counts can answer two questions. Compare death risks between the placebo and treatment groups, then compare the proportions given placebo among patients who died and those who recovered.

contingency_table
           
            Cured Dead
  Placebo       6   10
  Treatment    12    8
t(contingency_table)
       
        Placebo Treatment
  Cured       6        12
  Dead       10         8
p <- prop.table(contingency_table, margin = 1)
q <- prop.table(t(contingency_table), margin = 1)
round(rbind(
  `Death by group` = c(
    RR = p['Placebo', 'Dead'] / p['Treatment', 'Dead'],
    OR = (p['Placebo', 'Dead'] / p['Placebo', 'Cured']) /
      (p['Treatment', 'Dead'] / p['Treatment', 'Cured'])
  ),
  `Placebo by outcome` = c(
    RR = q['Dead', 'Placebo'] / q['Cured', 'Placebo'],
    OR = (q['Dead', 'Placebo'] / q['Dead', 'Treatment']) /
      (q['Cured', 'Placebo'] / q['Cured', 'Treatment'])
  )
), 2)
                     RR  OR
Death by group     1.56 2.5
Placebo by outcome 1.67 2.5

Why does transposing the table change the RR while the OR stays the same?

Interpreting risk and odds ratios

  • RR cannot be used in “case-control studies”, when you know the outcome of the exposure (treatment)

Since we already know the outcomes and the number of “patients” with each outcome we included in our study. Thus, calculating RISK is meaningless. In such a study, we can only calculate the ‘risk of TREATMENT in the case of CURED’ but not the other way around.

  • OR may overstate the effect (in both directions) and RR may be more “conservative”
mat <- matrix(c(45,55,10,90), 
             nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE,
             dimnames = list(c("A", "B"), c("+", "-"))) |> addmargins()
mat
     +   - Sum
A   45  55 100
B   10  90 100
Sum 55 145 200
RR_1 <- (mat[1,1]/mat[1,3])/(mat[2,1]/mat[2,3])
OR_1 <- (mat[1,1]/mat[1,2])/(mat[2,1]/mat[2,2])
paste0('RR = ', round(RR_1, 6))
[1] "RR = 4.5"
paste0('OR = ', round(OR_1, 6))
[1] "OR = 7.363636"
  • For rare events RR \(\approx\) OR
mat <- matrix(c(1,999,17,983), 
             nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE,
             dimnames = list(c("A", "B"), c("+", "-"))) |> addmargins()
mat
     +    -  Sum
A    1  999 1000
B   17  983 1000
Sum 18 1982 2000
RR_2 <- (mat[1,1]/mat[1,3])/(mat[2,1]/mat[2,3])
OR_2 <- (mat[1,1]/mat[1,2])/(mat[2,1]/mat[2,2])
paste0('RR = ', round(RR_2, 6))
[1] "RR = 0.058824"
paste0('OR = ', round(OR_2, 6))
[1] "OR = 0.057881"
  • \(RR\) = \(OR\) = 1 if two variables are not associated

Pearson’s correlation

Pearson’s correlation coefficient measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.

Based on counting distances of each value (point) from the average value of variable (e.g. \(X\) or \(Y\))

Formula

  • Pearson’s correlation coefficient:

    \(r = \dfrac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2}} \in [-1;1]\)

Interpretation

  • \(−1\) indicates a perfect negative (inverse) linear relationship.

  • \(+1\) indicates a perfect positive linear relationship.

  • \(0\) indicates no linear relationship.

Calculation

cor() returns the sample correlation \(r\) for two vectors. With a numeric data frame, it returns a correlation matrix.

cor(plant_height_df$Sunlight_hrs_per_day, plant_height_df$Plant_height)
[1] 0.7294685
# cor(iris %>%select(where(is.numeric)))

cor.test() reports the sample estimate, a 95% confidence interval for the population Pearson correlation \(\rho\), and a p-value for \(H_0:\rho=0\).

cor.test(plant_height_df$Sunlight_hrs_per_day,

Choosing a rank correlation

The problems with Pearson correlation are that it is not suitable for “strange” non-linear relationships, non-normally distributed traits and data with outliers.

Kendall’s \(\tau\)

  • better when ranks are repeated
cor(grades_math_phys, method = 'kendall')
         math     phys
math 1.000000 0.576624
phys 0.576624 1.000000

Spearman’s \(\rho\)

  • Suitable for non-linear relationships

  • Less sensitive to outliers

  • Calculated based on ranks

cor(grades_math_phys, method = 'spearman')
          math      phys
math 1.0000000 0.6775545
phys 0.6775545 1.0000000

Pooled and within-group correlations

In iris, how does the association between sepal width and petal length change when we compare flowers within the same species?

ggplot(iris, aes(Sepal.Width, Petal.Length, colour = Species)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(x = 'Sepal width (cm)', y = 'Petal length (cm)')
cor(iris$Sepal.Width, iris$Petal.Length)
[1] -0.4284401
iris |>
  group_by(Species) |>
  summarise(r = cor(Sepal.Width, Petal.Length))
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  Species        r
  <fct>      <dbl>
1 setosa     0.178
2 versicolor 0.561
3 virginica  0.401

The pooled correlation is negative, and all three within-species correlations are positive. Which estimate answers a question about flowers of the same species?

Conclusions

  1. Correlation does not imply causation
    • Website with numerous spurious correlations
  2. Correlation indicates presence of a relationship (association)
  3. Absence of correlation indicates absence of a linear relationship
  4. Absence of correlation does not indicate absence of a relationship (association)
